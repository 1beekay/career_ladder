# Using This Ladder

A ladder document is the easy part. The value comes from how you run it. This is the operating process I have used, plus the failure modes I have watched orgs fall into.

## Rolling it out

Do not ship a ladder as a surprise. The sequence that works:

1. **Draft with your senior people, not just managers.** Staff and Principal engineers must recognize themselves in the IC track or they will dismiss it, loudly.
2. **Level everyone privately before publishing.** Managers level their teams against the draft, then calibrate across teams. Find the painful mismatches before the document is public, not after.
3. **Publish with comp bands attached, or say plainly when that is coming.** A ladder without comp attached reads as homework. People want to know what the levels are worth.
4. **Set the expectation that titles do not change on day one.** You are describing reality, not reorganizing it.

## Calibration

Run calibration twice a year. Every manager presents their people against the ladder language, and other managers are expected to push. The two questions that do the work:

- "What is the evidence?" Specific projects, specific incidents, specific influence. Not vibes.
- "Would this person be at this level on your team?" Cross team consistency is the entire point of the exercise.

Write down the borderline cases and what was decided. Next cycle's arguments are this cycle's precedents.

## Promotions

A promotion is a statement that someone has already been operating at the next level for a sustained period, usually two quarters. The packet is short: the ladder language for the target level, the evidence against each dimension, written by the manager and sponsored in calibration.

Two rules that prevent most promotion drama:

- **No surprises in either direction.** If someone believes they are ready and their manager disagrees, that conversation happens early and explicitly, with the gaps named in writing.
- **The committee can say not yet, and that has to be survivable.** If a declined promotion is treated as a catastrophe, managers will stop putting honest cases forward and start gaming the process.

## AI and the ladder

AI proficiency is not a new dimension and it is not a level. Using AI is table stakes now, the way version control and tests are, so it lives in the execution baseline at E1, not in a promotion packet. What changes with level is not whether you use AI but what you do with it: at E1 it is leverage on your own work, at Staff it is a pattern others copy, at Principal and across management it is an operating model you build for the org.

Describe the behavior, never the tool. The tools will change; "can tell when the output is wrong" will not. And do not turn it into a metric to chase, for the same reason you do not build a leaderboard: the point is how people work, not a number they hit.

## Failure modes

**Level inflation.** The most common death. One generous calibration cycle becomes the new baseline, and three years later half the org is Staff. The defense is boring: hold the evidence bar in calibration, every cycle, even when it is uncomfortable.

**The ladder as a checklist.** Someone does one project per dimension and files for promotion. Levels describe how someone operates over time, not a scavenger hunt they completed once.

**Management as the reward.** If your best E3 gets offered a team as recognition, you have made two mistakes at once: you lost a strong builder and gained a reluctant manager. The IC track exists so that never has to happen.

**Copying a ladder you did not earn.** Including this one. Steal the structure, but rewrite the language until it describes your org's actual expectations. The arguments you have while rewriting it are where the alignment comes from.
